## Core contribution (one sentence)

This thesis builds and characterises a system that uses real-time driving telemetry from Assetto Corsa to decide when each piece of informational UI assistance should be faded out for a novice player, and reports how that system behaves in practice with a small group of novice drivers.

## Motivation

When novices learn a complex perceptual-motor task in a simulator — driving, flying, surgical training, teleoperation — informational UI overlays are often used to scaffold performance. A common question for designers is when and how to remove that scaffolding so the learner internalises the skill rather than depending on the overlay. In practice, withdrawal is usually binary and is decided by a trainer or by the learner. There is little work on having the system itself decide, from observed performance, when a specific piece of assistance is ready to be withdrawn for a specific learner.

The thesis investigates one approach to this question in one setting. Sim racing in Assetto Corsa is chosen because it offers detailed real-time telemetry through a documented interface, supports custom in-game UI through a plugin API, and involves a continuous perceptual-motor task with low ethical and physical risk. Whether the approach transfers to other domains is a question the thesis can motivate but cannot answer.

## Theoretical grounding

Four established ideas motivate the design.

**Cognitive load theory** suggests that informational overlays reduce extraneous load early in learning, but that persistent overlays can prevent the learner from forming the internal schemas that fluent performance depends on. Scaffolding should be present while load on the underlying skill is high and removed as schemas form.

**Scaffolding theory** (the Zone of Proximal Development and its later operationalisations) holds that support should be calibrated to current competence: present when the task is beyond independent capability, withdrawn as competence grows. The original formulation assumes a human tutor making the judgement. The thesis treats telemetry-derived competency assessment as a computational substitute for that judgement.

**Expertise research in driving and related perceptual-motor domains** shows a more nuanced picture than experts simply disengaging from visual cues. In real-world driving, expertise is linked to more efficient gaze behaviour and greater reliance on internalised anticipatory models. In sim racing specifically, however, Joyce et al. (2024) found the opposite of disengagement from the display: higher-skilled racers allocated relatively less overt attention to the track and proportionally more to heads-up display features than lower-skilled racers, who fixated more heavily on the track itself. Experts therefore appear to extract more, not less, from explicit display elements while driving at speed. The implication for fading is narrower than removing visual information wholesale: what should fade is the instructional scaffolding that substitutes for a skill not yet internalised — braking markers, a racing line — rather than the telemetry that experts continue to depend on. Fading aims to encourage formation of the internal model the scaffold previously stood in for, not to strip away all on-screen information.

**Motor-learning research on augmented feedback** is the oldest and most direct grounding, and an earlier version of this document missed it entirely. The *guidance hypothesis* (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter, 1984) holds that augmented feedback guides performance effectively while present but can block the information processing on which retention depends, so that learners come to rely on the feedback and their gains evaporate when it is removed. Two scheduling results follow from it and bear directly on this design: *reduced-frequency (faded) knowledge of results* — withdrawing feedback progressively across practice — improved retention relative to feedback on every trial (Winstein & Schmidt, 1990), and *bandwidth feedback* (Sherwood, 1988) provided feedback only when error exceeded a tolerance band, making feedback provision performance-contingent trial by trial. This literature reframes a finding cited below: the collapse Bugeja et al. (2017) observed when their real-time feedback was switched off is not a newly documented failure mode but a domain-specific replication of the guidance effect, four decades after it was named. It also supplies the strongest available support for the design hypothesis — that competence-contingent, gradual withdrawal should serve unassisted performance better than constant assistance — while the education-domain evidence on fading remains mixed (see below).

Taken together, these ideas motivate — but do not guarantee — a prediction: that scaffolding withdrawn contingently on demonstrated competence should support transfer better than scaffolding that is always present or never present. This prediction is not a settled consequence of scaffolding theory. The empirical record comparing fixed (always-on) and faded scaffolds is genuinely mixed: several studies report that fixed, sustained scaffolds yield better problem-solving and transfer outcomes than faded ones (Lee & Songer, 2004; Bulu & Pedersen, 2010; Jaakkola & Veermans, 2018; Tawfik et al., 2018), and a recent study of real-time adaptive scaffolding (Nickl et al., 2024) found no advantage for adaptive scaffolding over static or absent scaffolding. The direction of the effect is therefore an open empirical question rather than a logical certainty — which is what makes the comparison in RQ2 worth running. The theory also does not specify how contingency should be computed. The thesis contributes one mechanism — a multi-stage state machine with probe-and-revert behaviour — and characterises how it behaves with novice users. This design is also a direct response to a documented failure mode in prior sim-racing interventions: a telemetry-based real-time feedback system for novice drivers in Assetto Corsa (Bugeja et al., 2017) improved performance during training, but the gains dissipated as soon as the feedback was switched off — a lack of cognitive retention when drivers had to perform unassisted, and, read against the motor-learning literature above, a replication of the guidance effect rather than a new phenomenon. Probe-and-revert fading, which withdraws assistance gradually and restores it if performance degrades, is intended as a solution to exactly that collapse rather than a re-enactment of it.

## Related work and the engineering problem

Separated from the learning-science questions, the systems contribution addresses a single engineering problem: to build a controller that, in real time and independently for each UI element, infers from continuous driving telemetry whether a learner has internalised the skill a given piece of informational scaffolding stands in for, and then withdraws that scaffolding reversibly — restoring it if performance regresses — using an inspectable rule set rather than a learned policy. Prior work addresses every component of this but does not assemble them in this form.

**Telemetry-based coaching in sim racing** establishes both the need for fading and the metrics to drive it. Bugeja et al. (2017) built a telemetry-driven feedback system for Assetto Corsa and found that novice gains dissipated the moment feedback was disabled, attributing this to a lack of cognitive retention — the failure mode that motivates gradual, reversible withdrawal over a binary switch. Hojaji et al. applied machine learning to Assetto Corsa telemetry and identified the behaviours that separate fast from slow racers — trail braking, throttle-release timing, steering stability — which grounds the use of telemetry-derived competency metrics, most directly the braking-related ones. Joyce et al. (2024) showed through eye tracking that higher-skilled racers allocate proportionally less attention to the track and more to HUD elements, supporting the treatment of instructional track overlays and persistent HUD telemetry as distinct fading targets rather than a single class.

**Performance-contingent assistance** supplies the mechanical precedent for an assessment loop that drives the interface. Faber et al. built a medical-emergency simulator that toggled scaffolding from continuous interaction traces, enabling a supportive checklist when a systematicity score fell below a set threshold and surfacing procedural dialogs on tool warnings — performance-triggered support with no human in the loop. Ma et al. adapted AR-HUD driving warnings continuously from real-time vehicle kinematics using Ecological Interface Design, mapping interface changes onto skill-, rule-, and knowledge-based behaviour. Both adapt UI from system-assessed behaviour; neither withdraws scaffolding on demonstrated competence, and neither reverts.

**Adaptive training and augmented-feedback scheduling** is the prior art that comes closest to the engineering problem, and any honest gap statement must be written against it rather than around it. Kelley (1969) defined adaptive training as training in which the task — including augmented cueing — is adjusted automatically as a function of measured performance, and flight-simulation research in that tradition (e.g., Lintern & Gopher, 1978) withdrew augmented cues contingent on performance in continuous perceptual-motor tasks decades before any of the systems above. Bandwidth feedback (Sherwood, 1988) likewise makes feedback provision performance-contingent on every trial. Performance-contingent withdrawal of assistance in a continuous perceptual-motor task is therefore *not* novel in itself. What that literature does not contain: per-element granularity (a global difficulty or cueing parameter is adjusted, not several independent scaffolds with separate competency criteria), staged salience reduction, or explicit reversion semantics — withdrawal there is a schedule or a trial-level gate, not a reversible state machine with hysteresis, capped reversions, and censoring rules.

**Automated fading in education and intelligent tutoring** is the theory-to-systems lineage and the closest analogue to the staged controller used here. Zhang et al. implemented a three-stage fade-out — selective prompts, then gap-filling, then questioning — that parallels the full-salience, reduced-form, probe-withdrawal stages. Gidalevich & Kramarski found a graduated-reduction (faded) model produced better long-term retention and autonomy than fixed scaffolding, which sits opposite the fixed-favouring findings noted under theoretical grounding and places the direction of the effect in genuine dispute — the dispute RQ2 examines. These systems all operate over discrete, problem-based interactions; none addresses a continuous perceptual-motor task.

**The choice of a discrete, inspectable state machine** over a learned policy has direct support. Chrysafiadi et al. built a rule-based adaptation mechanism and justified avoiding machine learning on the grounds that such techniques demand large training data and learning time that real-time, individual adaptation cannot supply — the same constraint that rules out a learned controller for this task and population. The same work argues against leaving withdrawal to the user, since learners are poor judges of the challenge level matching their ability; this favours automating withdrawal from objective telemetry over self-report. That concern is adjacent to dynamic difficulty adjustment, which shares the goal of avoiding overload and frustration but adjusts game challenge rather than instructional scaffolding.

**The gap, stated against the strongest prior art:** performance-contingent withdrawal of assistance in continuous perceptual-motor tasks exists (adaptive training; bandwidth feedback), staged fading exists (intelligent tutoring, over discrete problem-based interactions), and telemetry-derived competency assessment in sim racing exists. What no prior system combines: (a) **per-element independence** — three scaffolds with distinct telemetry criteria fading on separate trajectories, where adaptive training adjusted a single global parameter; (b) **explicit reversibility** — a staged probe-and-revert state machine with asymmetric hysteresis, a reversion cap, and defined censoring semantics, where prior withdrawal is a one-way schedule or a per-trial gate; (c) **the fading target is the instructional cue itself** — world-anchored UI scaffolds standing in for an uninternalised skill — rather than the knowledge-of-results channel that the feedback-scheduling literature manipulates; and (d) **a fully inspectable, published rule set** characterised per-participant rather than evaluated only on aggregate outcomes. The contribution is this integration and its characterisation, not any single component — and explicitly not the idea of performance-contingent withdrawal, which is sixty years old.

## Research questions

**RQ1.** How does the fading state machine behave when used by novice players, and what fade trajectories does it produce across individuals and across UI elements?

**RQ2.** How does adaptive fading compare to static (always-on) UI assistance in shaping learning during training and in unassisted performance afterwards?

**RQ3.** How do players experience the fading process — workload, confidence, awareness of changes — and how do those experiences line up with the objective fade events the system records?

RQ1 treats the system as a contribution in its own right. RQ2 provides effect-size estimates with credible intervals, not significance tests, and compares the adaptive system as a whole against static assistance (see Evaluation constraints on the contingency–dose confound). RQ3 connects objective system behaviour to learner experience.

## Evaluation constraints (stated up front)

The realistic participant pool is approximately 15 people. The study uses a within-subjects design with two conditions on two different car-track pairs, counterbalanced via a 2×2 crossover (the full factorial of pair order × condition coupling, four sequences). This is the maximum statistical leverage the sample size will support, and it is still not enough for confirmatory between-condition tests.

The within-subjects design also carries a known cost: the skills being trained — braking discipline, apex awareness, corner-speed control — are largely track-general for novices, so generic improvement carries over into whichever car-track pair a participant drives second, regardless of condition. This is in tension with the thesis's own premise that these skills transfer. The counterbalancing balances order in expectation, but at n≈15 the imbalance in any individual cell is real and is not fully absorbed by a baseline covariate. The design therefore trades participant efficiency for condition contamination; the analysis models order explicitly rather than assuming it away (see Analysis), and the limitation is reported rather than hidden.

A second design limitation is stated in the same spirit. The adaptive and static conditions differ in two things at once: *contingency* (withdrawal is tied to demonstrated competence) and *dose* (adaptive participants receive less total scaffolding exposure). Isolating contingency would require a third, yoked condition — fading on a fixed schedule, or replaying another participant's fade timeline non-contingently — which a pool of ~15 cannot support. RQ2 therefore compares *the adaptive system as a whole* against static assistance; if a difference is observed, the design cannot attribute it to contingency rather than to reduced exposure. This is an experimental-design limitation, acknowledged and accepted as such, not a theoretical claim in need of literature support.

The thesis therefore frames itself as a **systems-and-characterisation study**:

- It can describe how the mechanism behaves in practice and produce per-participant trajectories.
- It can produce effect-size estimates with credible intervals for future powered work.
- It cannot make confirmatory claims that adaptive fading outperforms static assistance, and it does not try to.

This constraint is stated here rather than buried later because it shapes every downstream choice in the methodology.

## The system

The system has three loosely coupled parts: a telemetry-ingestion layer reading Assetto Corsa's shared-memory interface, a competency-assessment and fading-controller layer, and an in-game UI rendering layer.

### UI elements (three, deliberately scoped down)

- A world-anchored braking-point marker, projected onto the track surface at the expert braking point for each corner.
- A corner-speed indicator in the HUD.
- A world-anchored apex marker, projected onto the track at each corner's apex.

A dynamic racing line, a gear-shift indicator, and the delta-time bar carried in an earlier version of this design were considered and dropped. The racing line because rendering a continuously camera-tracking world-anchored line — hundreds of connected points redrawn every frame — is the most complex piece of UI work in the project, and its contribution overlaps with the braking-point and apex markers; the gear-shift indicator because it offers limited learning signal at the chosen vehicle and track difficulty; the delta-time bar because it is an expert feedback instrument rather than a scaffold a novice internalises and then sheds, which makes it a poor fit for a fading study. The braking-point and apex markers are world-anchored but discrete — each is a single projected point, not a continuous line — which keeps their rendering cheap where the racing line was not.

### World-anchored rendering (de-risked by a technical spike — resolved)

World-anchored rendering was the project's highest technical risk, because two of the three UI elements depend on it. It was therefore built first, as a throwaway spike, before any study infrastructure. **The spike passed**, but it overturned the rendering approach this document originally assumed, so the design is recorded here in its corrected form.

**What the spike disproved.** The original design assumed Assetto Corsa's legacy Python app API exposed a usable world-to-screen camera transform that could be read each frame and used to project a marker's world coordinate to screen pixels by hand. This is false. Stock AC Python has no such transform — it was a never-shipped feature request. The Custom Shaders Patch (CSP) extension does expose the camera matrices to Python, but the bindings proved fragile: matrices arrive column-major (transposed from the textbook layout), and under AC's bundled Python 3.3 the matrix elements are a custom numeric type whose `__float__` and `__repr__` raise on a single element, so the only reliable extraction was to `repr()` the whole tuple and parse the numbers back out. Worse, even when working, the Python path is a depth-less 2D overlay: it draws over intervening geometry and has to be manually culled once its world point passes behind the camera, and it can never be correctly occluded by hills, walls, or track furniture.

**What the spike adopted.** The marker is instead drawn with CSP's **Lua `render` API**, which draws primitives directly in 3D world space. World coordinates are handed to the engine, which projects them with the real scene camera during the transparent render pass. This inverts every Python problem at once:

- **No camera transform to fetch, parse, or transpose** — projection is engine-side.
- **Frame-staleness is impossible by construction** — the marker is drawn from the same camera, in the same pass, as the scene itself, so the "no lag to accumulate" property is now a structural guarantee rather than a hopeful prediction.
- **Lock at speed holds** — confirmed driving at a fixed anchor at 200+ km/h and swerving through it; the marker stays glued to its world point because it is part of the same render.
- **Depth testing and occlusion are free** — `render.setDepthMode(Normal)` gives true depth-buffered drawing, so markers are correctly hidden behind geometry with no manual culling. The depth-less-overlay limitation noted in the original design is gone, with no material override or extra toolchain.
- **Per-frame cost is negligible** — a handful of `render.*` calls per marker.

**The design consequence.** World-anchored rendering is implemented as a **CSP Lua app, and CSP is therefore a documented dependency of the study apparatus.** The thesis contribution — the *fading* of the cue — is unaffected by this change and lives in the same layer: fading is implemented by modulating the marker's `rgbm` alpha (and optionally its radius) as a function of the controller's stage, and the rendering call is identical whether the marker is at full salience, reduced salience, or withdrawn. The original design principle — keep the *rendering* swappable and the *fading* independent of it — is what made the Python-to-Lua swap cheap, and it still holds. Each marker's world position is precomputed once per corner from the expert reference (see Method); at run time the controller only sets alpha.

### Corner segmentation: manual annotation

Every per-corner mechanism in this design — reference table rows, evidence windows, marker placement — presupposes an answer to a question the engine does not provide: what counts as a corner, and where does a corner pass begin and end? With only two tracks in the study, this is solved by **manual annotation rather than automatic detection**. Automatic corner detection from telemetry is a research problem in its own right; for roughly 12–15 corners per track, annotation is a few hours of one-time work per track and more reliable than any detector would be.

Each track has a version-controlled annotation file with one record per corner: a corner ID; an entry gate and an exit gate expressed as normalized track-spline positions (Assetto Corsa exposes the car's normalized spline position directly, which makes gate-crossing detection trivial and robust to lateral position on the road); a corner-type tag (slow / medium / fast, for reporting); and per-element evaluation zones — a *braking zone* (the spline interval over which brake initiation is detected, ending at turn-in), an *apex gate* (the spline position at which lateral distance to the reference line is sampled), and a *corner-speed zone* (the interval over which minimum corner speed is taken). Compound sections are annotated explicitly rather than left to interpretation: a chicane or linked sequence sharing a single braking event is one compound corner with one braking zone and, where appropriate, multiple apex gates; corners with separate braking events are separate corners even when geometrically close. A **corner pass** is then defined mechanically — it begins when the car's spline position crosses the entry gate and ends at the exit gate, at which point the pass's metrics are computed and its validity flag set. The annotation files ship with the published dataset, so the segmentation is fully inspectable and reproducible.

### The fading mechanism

For each UI element independently, a three-stage state machine: full salience → reduced salience (lower opacity or simpler form) → probe withdrawal.

*[amended 2026-09-09]* **Reduced salience is a simpler form, not merely a lower opacity**, and the choice was made deliberately rather than left to implementation. The braking and apex markers reduce to their ground disc alone — the vertical stalk, the cross and the corner label are removed — and the corner-speed indicator reduces to its target number alone, dropping the corner label and the live or achieved speed. The reasoning is that reducing salience should reduce what the cue *tells* the learner, not just how brightly it says it: a dimmer copy of the same marker still hands over the corner identity and a precise point to aim at, and a dimmer copy of the full corner-speed readout still does the driver's comparing for them — and it is the comparing that has to be internalised. What survives the reduction is a positional hint; what is taken away is the reading. The alternative (the complete cue at lower alpha) is retained as a configurable option precisely because it is the control case for this argument. Because participants are asked about the fading in the RQ3 interview, the choice is a frozen, hashed configuration value rather than a property of the code. Transitions move **one stage at a time, in either direction**: forward when competence is demonstrated, backward when it degrades.

**Evidence is two-level: per-corner qualification, element-level quorum.** An earlier version of this design gated each stage transition on the competency criterion being met across three *consecutive laps*. This was abandoned: a mid-length circuit yields only one evidence event per lap, and a single bad pass resets the count to zero — too slow and too brittle for noisy novice driving. But the obvious replacement, pooling all corner passes into a single element-level rolling window, has the opposite flaw: the element could fade because the learner has mastered the easy corners while still failing the hard ones, an average that easy corners can carry. And pushing the state all the way down to individual corners — the marker disappearing at turn 3 while persisting at turn 9 — makes the interface incoherent and the participant's experience hard to interpret or ask about. The design therefore separates *evidence* from *presentation*:

- **Per-corner qualification (evidence).** For each annotated corner and each element, the corner becomes **qualified** when the element's criterion is met in **3 of that corner's last 4 passes**, and is **de-qualified** when the criterion fails on 2 consecutive passes. Qualification is computed continuously from telemetry regardless of whether the element is currently visible.
- **Element-level quorum (presentation).** An element advances a stage when **≥ 75% of the corners where it applies are simultaneously qualified**, and reverts one stage when the qualified share falls below **50%**. The element changes everywhere at once, so the display stays globally coherent while the evidence stays local — broad mastery is required, not a pooled average.
- **Re-qualification per stage.** On any stage **advance**, every corner's qualification state for that element resets to unqualified and its evidence window restarts; quorum must be rebuilt from passes driven *under the new salience level* before the next advance. Without this rule the mechanism is degenerate: the quorum condition that triggered full → reduced would still hold immediately afterwards, and the element would advance again on the very next evaluation — the reduced stage would be a cosmetic waypoint rather than a test. The reset is what makes each stage demand fresh evidence at the current level of support. Stage **reversions** do not reset qualification: the depleted evidence that fell below quorum is exactly what the learner must rebuild to advance again.

*[amended 2026-09-09]* Building the mechanism exposed two gaps in the rules above. Neither is a detail of implementation: each is a rule the machine needs in order to behave as this document describes, and each was found by running the state machine against a corpus of deliberately pathological input traces rather than by reasoning about it.

- **Quorum readiness.** The re-qualification reset sets every corner to unqualified, which drops the qualified share to zero — *below the revert threshold*. Stated as above, the mechanism therefore reverts every advance on the very next pass, and the reduced stage is unreachable rather than merely cosmetic. The rule that fixes it: an element's quorum is **not evaluated at all** until every applicable corner has accumulated enough passes in its current window to support a verdict. It applies symmetrically to advances and reversions, it errs in the conservative direction the design asks for, and it has a second benefit that was not the reason for it — it makes the probe's "3 laps with quorum maintained" a judgement on rebuilt evidence rather than on an empty window, which is what the probe was always supposed to test.

- **One regression, one stage.** After a reversion the element sits one stage lower still holding the sub-threshold share that caused the reversion, so the next pass reverts it again. A single deep collapse therefore cascaded two stages in two passes, and — because a probe reversion always dropped straight through reduced to full — the reversion cap could never be reached at all, making rule 2 of the probe rules below unreachable in practice. The rule that fixes it: after a *reversion*, the revert check is **disarmed** until the qualified share recovers to the threshold; after an *advance* it stays armed. The asymmetry is the point. One regression should cost one stage, so the same depleted evidence must not be spent twice; but a *premature* advance must still be undoable when the learner cannot produce the evidence at the new salience level, which is the whole of probe-and-revert.

Both rules are consequences of the design as stated, not additions to it — each is what "reverts one stage when the qualified share falls below 50%" has to mean once the re-qualification reset exists alongside it. They are documented here because the mechanism is a first-class contribution of this thesis, and a mechanism whose stated rules do not produce its described behaviour is not one.

A lap generates 10–15 evidence events (one per corner), de-qualification is fast where regression is real, and an isolated mistake dents one corner's window instead of erasing everything. The asymmetric quorum thresholds (advance at 75%, revert below 50%) are deliberate hysteresis, preventing flicker at the boundary. One consequence of the de-qualification rule is acknowledged rather than hidden: a qualified corner that fails every *other* pass never accumulates two consecutive failures and therefore never de-qualifies, despite a sustained 50% failure rate. The re-qualification reset bounds how long such a corner can coast (its state is wiped at the next advance), and the probe stage is the behavioural backstop; the case is nonetheless encoded as an explicit golden-trace test in the implementation plan rather than left to chance.

**Probe rules, stated precisely.** The probe-withdrawal stage is governed by four explicit rules:

1. **Duration.** A probe lasts **3 full laps** with the element hidden. Per-corner qualification continues to be evaluated throughout — withdrawal removes the scaffold, never the measurement.
2. **Reversion.** If the qualified share falls below 50% during the probe, the element reverts one stage, to reduced salience. The learner can re-enter the probe by re-reaching quorum. At most **2 reversion cycles per element per block** are allowed; after the second reversion the element stays at reduced salience for the remainder of the block and is logged as such. The cap prevents oscillation, which would be both confusing to the learner and uninterpretable as data.
3. **Permanence.** If the probe completes its 3 laps with quorum maintained, the element is removed for the remainder of the block. "Permanent" is explicitly **block-scoped**: every block begins at full salience, and no fade state carries across blocks or sessions. (In this study the cross-block case is moot — each participant has exactly one adaptive training block — but the scoping is stated so the mechanism is fully defined rather than defined-by-accident.)
4. **Censoring.** If a block ends mid-probe, the probe is logged as **incomplete (right-censored)**: in RQ1's probe-outcome tabulation it counts as neither held nor reverted, and the element's final state is recorded as in-probe.

**Lap-budget feasibility is checked by simulation before any participant is run.** A small simulation generates synthetic novice metric streams (noisy early, improving along a plausible learning curve, with lap-to-lap noise) and runs them through the state machine. All controller parameters above — the 3-of-4 qualification window, the 2-failure de-qualification rule, the 75% / 50% quorum thresholds, the 3-lap probe, the reversion cap — are defaults to be tuned in that simulation, not commitments. The arithmetic the defaults must satisfy: a fast-improving learner should traverse the full machine in roughly 9–11 laps — about 3–4 laps to qualify broadly at full salience, 3–4 more to *re-qualify* at reduced salience (the dwell is enforced by the re-qualification rule, not assumed), then the 3-lap probe — which fits a 13–15-lap training block with margin for noise; a non-improving learner should never leave full salience. The simulation confirms the budget closes before any participant time is spent, and the resulting figure documents that the controller's behaviour is designed rather than accidental.

*[amended 2026-09-09]* **The simulation has been run** (2,800 synthetic streams across seven learner types and both car-track pairs). The budget closes, and one parameter moved to close it: the corner-speed tolerance floor, from a provisional 3.0 to 4.0 km/h, which was the single binding constraint. Every other default above survived calibration unchanged. Three results bear on what this document claims. A typical improver traverses two of the three elements within a block, not all three — **partial traversal is the expected case**, and the arithmetic above should be read as describing the fastest learners rather than the median one. About a third of typical improvers finish mid-probe, so right-censoring is a design feature of the block length rather than an occasional accident. And the reversion cap was reached by none of the 2,800 streams, so it remains a safeguard whose necessity is argued rather than demonstrated. The verdict is only as good as the synthetic learner it was run against, and only one of that model's parameters has an empirical anchor; the pilot is what tests the assumption.

### Competency metrics

Each element has one or more telemetry-derived metrics targeting the skill it scaffolds, all defined relative to an expert reference built from multiple recorded laps (a tolerance band, not a point target):

- **Braking-point marker — a two-gate, per-pass criterion.** A single corner pass satisfies the braking criterion only when both gates hold *for that pass*: brake-initiation distance within a tolerance band of the expert braking point (*accuracy*), **and** brake-initiation distance within a named *consistency delta* of the rolling mean of that corner's recent passes — the same passes that form the qualification window (*consistency*). Brake-pressure modulation is logged descriptively but does not gate. An earlier version of this design defined consistency as a variance threshold computed *over* the qualification window. That definition was withdrawn for two reasons: it is logically incompatible with the qualification rule, which requires a per-pass boolean ("met in 3 of the last 4 passes") and cannot consume a statistic that only exists at the window level; and a variance estimated from 3–4 passes carries two to three degrees of freedom, making the gate a coin flip on noise in both directions. The per-pass delta preserves the intent at the unit the state machine actually operates on. Consistency alone remains insufficient on purpose — a learner who brakes reliably 30 m too early sits tight to their own rolling mean (passing consistency) but outside the accuracy band, so the marker stays. Requiring accuracy *and* consistency makes the trigger track the *correct* braking point rather than mere repeatability — though what the gates can certify is deliberately limited; see below.
- **Corner-speed indicator** — minimum corner-speed deviation from the expert speed envelope.
- **Apex marker** — RMS lateral deviation from the expert line over a short spline window centred on the apex gate (the half-width is a named annotation constant). A single-point sample at the gate — the definition an earlier draft of the evaluation plan carried — is noisy and gameable: a learner can clip one point while missing the line entirely. Integrating over a window measures the path, not a coincidence.

The metrics are kept deliberately distinct — longitudinal control for the braking point, a target value for corner speed, lateral path accuracy for the apex — so the three elements can in principle fade on independent trajectories rather than moving together.

Tolerance bands are not the raw variance of the reference laps. An AI driver has no biological motor noise, while even expert human performance never eliminates variability — inherent motor noise in the human nervous system persists at all skill levels (Cohen & Sternad, 2009) — so bands derived directly from the AI's lap-to-lap spread would be impossibly narrow for any human. Each band is therefore **floored and source-dependent**: `band_m = max(σ_ref,m × EVM_source, floor_m)`. The multiplier — the **Expertise Variance Multiplier (EVM)** — compensates for the reference's missing motor noise, with a provisional default of **EVM_AI = 3.0** for AI-sourced corners, calibrated empirically in the pilot by comparing real human variance against the AI baseline. Two refinements guard the structure itself. First, the **absolute per-metric floor** exists because the AI is consistent by construction: at some corners σ_ref may be near zero, and any multiplier of nearly-nothing is still nearly-nothing — without a floor the band is hostage to an arbitrarily small baseline. Second, corners whose reference is **human-substituted** (see Method) already contain human motor variability in σ_ref; applying the AI multiplier there would count motor noise twice, so those corners take a smaller multiplier, **EVM_human** (provisional default 1.25), calibrated alongside EVM_AI. Naming the parameters rather than burying them follows the precedent of Bugeja et al. (2017), whose Assetto Corsa feedback system built explicit `tolerance` parameters into its detection algorithms (e.g., slip-ratio and racing-line tolerances) and set them by empirical observation.

### What the gates certify — and what only the probe can

The competency gates are computed during *guided* acquisition — while the scaffold is visible — and during-practice performance is a flawed proxy for learning. Soderstrom and Bjork (2015) draw exactly this distinction: learning must be distinguished from performance, where performance is the temporary fluctuation in behaviour observable during acquisition, and what is measured during training is often an unreliable index of whether durable learning has taken place. The same literature cuts deeper for this design: guidance reliably reduces errors while it is present, but when assessment is conducted without it, learners often perform worse than if they had practised unguided — that is, learners can satisfy visible-assistance criteria precisely *because* the assistance is visible. An earlier version of this document claimed that the two-gate braking criterion makes the fade "certify that the learner has internalised the correct braking point." On this evidence, that claim was an overstep, and it is withdrawn.

The corrected position: the telemetry gates are a **heuristic trigger, not a measurement of learning**. Their job is to decide *when an unassisted probe is worth running*. The probe-withdrawal stage is the actual test of internalisation — a short-term retention test under exactly the condition that matters, performance without the scaffold — and reversion is the system's built-in admission that the gates' inference was premature for a given learner. This is why the mechanism is probe-and-revert rather than threshold-and-remove: the design assumes from the outset that during-practice metrics will sometimes certify performance that has not yet become learning, and corrects for it structurally rather than statistically.

### Why a discrete state machine rather than a learned policy

Training a continuous probabilistic controller (MDP, POMDP, knowledge tracing) would require data that doesn't exist for this task and population. A discrete state machine is also inspectable, which matters because the design choices themselves are part of the contribution; a learned policy would hide them.

## Method summary

**Participants.** Approximately 15 novice sim racers (under 20 hours of sim racing across any title, no league participation). Pre-study questionnaire records driving history and game experience as potential moderators.

**Expert reference.** The competency thresholds and the markers' world coordinates are both derived from a **competent, consistent reference — not an optimal one.** The reference is built from the Assetto Corsa AI driver run at 95–100% strength for 10–15 laps on each car-track pair, in the same fixed setup participants use, logged through the study's own telemetry pipeline. This choice is deliberate: public internet hotlaps are alien-tier laps on unknown, optimised setups, so tolerance bands anchored to them would never admit a novice and nothing would ever fade; they also typically provide a single lap, which cannot supply the variance estimate a tolerance band needs. The AI reference is reproducible, fully documented ("AC AI, strength X%, n laps, fixed setup"), consistent by construction across multiple laps, and the AI line conveniently also supplies the per-corner world coordinates for the markers. Logging it doubles as an end-to-end test of the telemetry pipeline. The AI's known weaknesses are confronted up front rather than discovered later: the stock AI brakes conservatively and does not trail brake — precisely the behaviours Hojaji et al. identify as discriminating experts from novices. Validation against a human reference was planned for exactly this reason, and **it was not achieved.** No experienced sim racer was available, and the researcher is a beginner; a reference recorded by a beginner would not be a weaker expert reference but a *novice* one, and substituting it would move the target toward novice performance, so the scaffold would fade when a participant drove like a beginner — inverting the intervention's premise. The reference is therefore **AI-sourced at every corner**, and the claim that its brake points fall within a competent human's tolerance is **not established**; this is reported as a limitation rather than assumed away. Three things bound the cost. First, the AI's conservatism runs in the *same direction* as novice error — both brake earlier than an expert — so an AI reference is a less demanding target than a human expert's would be, which is the safe direction for a scaffold a novice has to reach. Second, the tolerance bands are floor-dominated in practice: the AI's measured per-corner σ (0.005–0.88 m, 0.20–0.41 km/h on the first recorded pair) sits far below the per-metric floors, so any AI bias smaller than a floor is absorbed by the band rather than transmitted to the criterion. Third, the pilot (Gate G2) compares real novice per-corner distributions against the reference and finalises those floors, which is where an unreachable or mis-centred reference surfaces empirically. The per-corner comparison-and-substitution machinery is built and tested, so if an experienced driver becomes available before the main study, this validation is a re-run rather than a rebuild.

**Pilot.** With the simulation and the AI-derived reference in place, the pilot's job shrinks to a sanity check: confirm that real novice telemetry behaves roughly like the synthetic streams the thresholds were tuned against, and catch gross calibration errors before the main run. Two pilot participants, one session each; they may fall outside the main recruitment criteria if needed, and are not counted in the main sample.

**Apparatus.** Fixed force-feedback wheel, pedals with load-cell brake, fixed display, identical seating across participants. Custom Shaders Patch is a required software dependency of the rendering layer (see World-anchored rendering) and is held at a fixed version across all participants.

**Cars and tracks.** Two car-track pairs of moderate handling complexity (road or touring cars on mid-length circuits with varied corner types). Distinct enough to limit skill carryover between them, similar enough that the same UI elements and metrics apply. Residual carryover is expected and is handled in analysis rather than assumed away.

**Sessions.** Three sessions per participant. Session 1: familiarisation and baseline laps on both pairs. Session 2: training under both conditions on both pairs (crossover-sequence assignment of condition order and pair order). Session 3: unassisted performance block on both pairs. Optional retention probe 5–7 days later where availability permits — treated as exploratory, since it will likely yield too few participants to report as a primary result.

**Outcome measures.**

- *Primary (RQ2):* mean lap time and lap-time variability in the unassisted performance block. The word *transfer* is deliberately avoided throughout: session 3 tests the same corners on the same tracks with assistance removed, which is a retention / unassisted-performance test, not transfer in the learning-science sense. Transfer to novel corners or tracks is out of scope for this thesis and is named as future work.
- *System behaviour (RQ1):* per-participant fade-event timelines, probe-withdrawal outcomes per element, distribution of fade timings across participants.
- *Secondary:* sector-level performance, input smoothness, off-track count, retention performance.
- *Subjective (RQ3):* NASA-TLX after each training block, a brief confidence and perceived-dependence questionnaire, and a short semi-structured interview at the end of session 3.

**Analysis.**

- *RQ1:* descriptive presentation of per-participant fade trajectories. Aggregate patterns shown as distributions rather than as group means. Atypical cases reported individually.
- *RQ2:* Bayesian mixed-effects model on lap-level observations — condition is *crossed* with participant, not nested — with a random intercept and condition slope per participant, baseline lap time as a covariate, pair as a fixed effect (two levels cannot support a variance estimate), and **condition order and pair order included as fixed effects** to absorb the carryover the within-subjects design introduces. At n≈15 the posteriors on the order terms will be wide; including them nonetheless shows the confound was modelled rather than ignored. Because both training blocks share a session, the order terms also absorb mean fatigue, but a fatigue × condition interaction is unidentifiable at this n and is reported as a limitation rather than modelled. Posterior distributions over the adaptive-versus-static difference are reported with credible intervals. Priors are stated in the methods chapter.
- *RQ3:* thematic summary of interview content, organised by predefined themes (workload, confidence, awareness of fading) plus any themes that emerge clearly across participants. Full formal thematic analysis with double coding is out of scope; the qualitative arm provides triangulation, not standalone qualitative results.

## Deliverables

- The working system: telemetry pipeline, competency-assessment and fading controller, three in-game UI overlays (CSP Lua rendering layer), logging infrastructure.
- A characterisation dataset with per-tick telemetry, lap summaries, fade events, and questionnaire responses.
- Per-participant learning trajectories (RQ1), effect-size estimates with credible intervals for the comparison (RQ2), and a thematic summary of participant experiences (RQ3).
- The written thesis, with a focused literature review, system documentation, evaluation results, and a discussion of design implications and limitations.

## What this thesis will not claim

- That adaptive telemetry-driven fading produces statistically significant improvements over static assistance.
- That findings generalise beyond the cars, tracks, and population tested.
- That the chosen thresholds, fade schedule, or UI choices are optimal.
- That the system is validated. The evaluation characterises behaviour; validation in a strong sense needs a future powered study.
